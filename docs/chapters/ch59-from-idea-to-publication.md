# Chapter 59. From Idea to Publication: Practice, Ethics, Biosecurity, and Independent Research

!!! abstract "Chapter at a glance"
    **Motivation.** The chapters before this one taught how to understand, evaluate, and generate research in AI for biology. This chapter covers what separates a good analysis from a *contribution to the field*: designing a study so its result is credible, writing the paper so its claims match its evidence, releasing data and models responsibly, and recognizing when a project carries risks (to people whose data are used, to patients, or through dual-use) that must be addressed before, not after, publication. It closes with eight capstone projects that together form a path from reader to independent researcher.
    **Prerequisites.** All of Parts I–IX in outline; Chapters 1, 25, 26, 43, 55–58 especially.
    **You will be able to:** (1) design a study with a pre-specified analysis, locked splits, ceilings, baselines, and a reproducible pipeline; (2) write a paper whose abstract verbs match the rung of its evidence; (3) handle human-subject data, equity, and clinical-translation questions competently; (4) perform a dual-use assessment and choose proportionate release practices; (5) plan and execute a twelve-week capstone with a claim–evidence table as the central deliverable.

## 59.1 The lifecycle of a study

A study moves through stages, and each stage has its own characteristic way to fail.

| Stage | Output | Typical failure | Safeguard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Idea | Attack Sheet (Ch. 55) | Solution-first | Diagnose gaps first |
| Triage | Ranked portfolio (Ch. 56) | No kill criteria | EIG per cost; pre-mortem |
| Toy / simulation | Known-answer demonstration | Skipped | A toy where the answer is known (Chs. 24–31) |
| Pilot on real data | Ceiling, baselines, leakage audit | Test data touched | Lock the split; baseline ladder |
| Full study | Pre-specified analysis, error bars | Forking paths | Registered analysis plan |
| Writing | Claim–evidence table → paper | Abstract exceeds evidence | Rung-calibrated claims |
| Review / revision | Responses, extra experiments | Defensive framing | Treat reviewers as adversarial tests |
| Release | Code, data, weights, documentation | Irreproducible; or unsafe | Release plan and risk assessment |

## 59.2 Designing a credible study

### 59.2.1 Pre-specification

Before looking at test results write down: the primary question and metric; the dataset and the **locked split** (including how near-duplicates are removed; Chapters 24, 27, 28, 43); the baselines (a *ladder*, Chapter 29) and their tuning budgets; the ceiling (replicate reliability, Chapter 1 and 25); the unit of replication and number of units; the statistical analysis (including how multiple comparisons are handled); and the decision rule for success. This does not forbid exploration; it labels *which analyses were planned* and which were exploratory, so that readers can weigh them.

### 59.2.2 Power and effect sizes

For a comparison between methods on $n$ independent units (proteins, donors, loci, perturbations) with paired differences of standard deviation $s$, a mean improvement $\Delta$ is detectable with probability $1-\beta$ at level $\alpha$ when
$$
n\ \gtrsim\ \Big(\frac{(z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta})\,s}{\Delta}\Big)^2 .
$$
For $\alpha=0.05$ and power 0.8, $(1.96+0.84)^2\approx7.8$, so $n\approx7.8\,s^2/\Delta^2$. A benchmark with 40 proteins and $s=0.15$ in Spearman correlation can detect a mean improvement of about $0.15\sqrt{7.8/40}\approx0.066$; the typical gains reported between neighboring models (0.02–0.04) are below that threshold, which is why *paired bootstrap over units*, and honest statements of what cannot be resolved, matter. When the *effective* number of units is smaller than the nominal one (clusters of homologs; donors; analog series), use the effective number.

### 59.2.3 Engineering for reproducibility

* **Version everything**: data (hashes, snapshots), code (commit), environment (container or lock-file), random seeds, and model checkpoints.
* **Separate the pipeline stages** (data preparation, splitting, training, evaluation) so that the split is produced once, stored, and never regenerated by accident.
* **Track experiments** with parameters and metrics; save *all* runs, not the best.
* **Test the code**: unit tests for data transformations (shapes; alignment of labels; the leak tests of Chapter 28), and a small end-to-end test on toy data with a known answer.
* **Budget compute** with the $6ND$ estimate (Chapter 56) and plan seeds; a result that cannot be replicated across seeds is not a result.
* **Report at the right level**: ceilings, baselines, error bars over the right unit, stratified results, and failure cases.

Reporting standards help: **datasheets for datasets** (Gebru et al., 2021), **model cards** (Mitchell et al., 2019), and the **DOME** recommendations for supervised machine learning in biology, covering Data, Optimization, Model, and Evaluation (Walsh et al., 2021).

## 59.3 Writing the paper

**Start from the claim–evidence table** (Chapter 57). Each row becomes a result paragraph and a figure; claims without evidence are removed or reworded; evidence without a claim goes to the supplement. A strong AI-for-biology paper has:

1. **A one-sentence claim** at the *rung* the evidence supports (C1: "predicts held-out…", C2: "…under X shift", C3: "…the model's computation reflects…", C4: "…designed… tested prospectively").
2. **A problem statement with an operational metric** and the *noise ceiling*.
3. **Baselines** on the ladder, tuned as carefully as the proposed method.
4. **Splits that match the claim**: family, cluster, donor, ancestry, time.
5. **Ablations** that identify which component matters.
6. **Error analysis**: where does it fail, and why (stratified; Chapter 55's A10 pass).
7. **Limitations** stated as testable boundaries, not as boilerplate.
8. **A reproducibility statement** that lets a reader regenerate the main table.

**Language discipline.** *Predicts* (C1–C2), *is associated with* (observational), *is consistent with* (a mechanism hypothesis), *causes* or *drives* (intervention), *designs* (prospective, with controls), *understands* (do not). Avoid *novel* as an adjective (the reader decides). Prefer "we find" over "we show" when the evidence is a single dataset.

**Preprints and review.** Post a preprint early to invite scrutiny but state its status. In review, treat a reviewer's objection as a *free adversarial test*: run the requested control even if you believe it is unnecessary; if it changes nothing, it strengthens the paper. Publish **negative results** when they are informative (powered, with ceilings and baselines).

**Authorship and credit.** Credit data generators, tool builders, and those who ran the evaluation (CRediT taxonomy); cite the datasets and software you use; cite the *benchmarks and negative results* that shaped your work.

## 59.4 Data, consent, privacy, and equity

**Human data.** Biological datasets of people are *personal*. Responsibilities include: use only data you are authorized to use under its consent terms and data-access agreement; respect controlled-access repositories; do not attempt re-identification. Genotype data are intrinsically identifying: surnames and other attributes of anonymous research participants were inferred from short-tandem-repeat profiles and public genealogy databases (Gymrek et al., 2013) [[E]], and aggregate summary statistics can sometimes reveal membership in a study (Homer et al., 2008) [[E]]. Treat individual-level genotype, expression, and especially phased long-read data as sensitive; apply the minimum necessary access, secure storage, and release only data that the consent covers.

**Equity and representation.** Cohorts and benchmarks are dominated by European-ancestry individuals (Chapter 26). Models built on them perform worse, sometimes much worse, in other populations (polygenic scores lose accuracy continuously with genetic distance; Ding et al., 2023) [[S]], which makes unevaluated deployment an equity risk. Evaluate across ancestry as a continuum; report subgroup performance; and collaborate with, and share benefit with, the communities whose data are used. The **CARE principles** (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics) for Indigenous data governance complement the FAIR principles (findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable) for data stewardship (Carroll et al., 2020).

**Clinical translation.** A model that predicts well retrospectively is a hypothesis about a clinical tool. Before use in care, prospective validation, calibration within subgroups, the decision it informs, its failure consequences, and regulatory status (software as a medical device) all matter. Report *net benefit* at the clinically relevant threshold, not only AUROC; ensure that the model does not encode access-to-care biases (a label that is "diagnosed with" reflects access as well as biology).

**Animals and environment.** Experiments that use animals, field samples, or organisms with ecological impact require review, minimization (the 3 Rs: replace, reduce, refine), and a justification that the planned computational triage reduces the experimental burden.

## 59.5 Dual-use and biosecurity

Some AI for biology can help bad actors as well as good ones. This section is about *proportionate, evidence-based risk management* for researchers; it does not provide, and the book does not contain, operational detail for harming people.

### 59.5.1 What is the risk, and what is the evidence?

Generative biological models can propose novel sequences, including variants of harmful proteins or viral genomes. Two lines of evidence are relevant to a reasonable assessment:

* **Design capability is real and growing.** Language-model-guided design of whole bacteriophage genomes (Evo 1 and Evo 2) produced about 300 synthesized genomes of which 16 were viable phages (King et al., *Science*, August 2026; Chapter 1) [[S]]; structure-aware generative tools can design binders and enzymes (Chapter 36). These systems *target bacteria*, not humans, but they demonstrate the capability class.
* **Safeguards can be circumvented, and can also be patched.** The *Paraphrase Project* (Wittmann et al., *Science*, 2 October 2025) used open-source generative protein design tools to produce roughly 76,000 variants of 72 harmful proteins and tested whether commercial DNA-synthesis screening software flagged them; screening flagged some but not all, and about a quarter of the best-scoring designs evaded detection initially; after three of the four screening providers deployed updates, about 3% of top-ranked designs were missed [[S]]. The study is both a demonstration of a vulnerability (a "zero-day" in screening that relied on sequence similarity) and an example of *coordinated disclosure and patching* in the cybersecurity style.

### 59.5.2 Mitigations that exist, and their limits

* **Training-data curation.** The developers of Evo 2 and ESM3 withheld sequences of viruses that infect eukaryotes, and others, from training; the data exclusion reduced the models' performance on virus-related fitness tasks, which suggests that curation is *partially effective* [[S]]. However, studies of **adversarial fine-tuning** of open-weight genome models suggest that capabilities removed by data filtering may be restorable with modest additional training on the excluded data, so filtering is *not* sufficient for open weights [[S]] (the evidence base is young [[P]]).
* **Staged and gated release.** Release capabilities through APIs with monitoring and access controls; release weights only after evaluation; withhold artifacts (specific sequences, optimized designs) that provide the most uplift.
* **Evaluation and red-teaming** before release, using biosecurity experts, and publication of *evaluations of safeguards*.
* **DNA-synthesis screening** (customer screening and sequence screening by providers; international consortia and national frameworks), improved by function-aware rather than only sequence-similarity-based screening, which is the lesson of the Paraphrase Project.
* **Community norms and commitments**, for example the 2024 community statement on responsible AI for protein design signed by many researchers, and journal and funder policies requiring dual-use assessments.
* **Institutional review**: biosafety and dual-use research committees.

### 59.5.3 A decision framework for researchers

1. **Characterize the capability** you are building: *what could this model do that, in the hands of a malicious actor, increases the risk of serious harm relative to what is already possible?* Distinguish **uplift** (the model does something substantially harder to do otherwise) from **convenience**.
2. **Characterize the agent of harm**: a design of a harmless bacterial phage is different from a pathogen of concern. If the work touches pathogens, toxins, or their enhancement, **consult biosafety and biosecurity experts before** collecting data, training models, or publishing.
3. **Estimate counterfactual impact**: is the capability available via existing open tools? Would your release materially lower the barrier?
4. **Choose proportionate controls**: data exclusion; withholding sensitive artifacts; API-only access; delayed publication with coordinated disclosure; reporting evaluation results *without* the optimized sequences.
5. **Document the assessment** and decisions in the paper's ethics and release statement; engage journals early.
6. **Prioritize defensive uses**: screening, detection, countermeasure design, surveillance, and open evaluation of safeguards.
7. **Revisit** the assessment when capabilities change (a larger model, a new modality, fine-tuning results).

!!! lens "Research lens: biosecurity as an evaluation problem"
    Biosecurity assessment has the same structure as the rest of this book: define the *capability of concern* operationally, build an evaluation that measures it (with ceilings and baselines), and report results honestly (including negative ones). A **safeguard** is a hypothesis ("this filter prevents X"), to be tested adversarially (A5: a red-team is an evaluation attack). Uncertainty is substantial; policy should combine precaution for severe, irreversible harms with openness for the large benefits (vaccines, diagnostics, enzymes, crop resilience).

## 59.6 Becoming an independent researcher

**Choose problems** by the triage of Chapter 56 *and* by comparative advantage: where do your skills, data access, and collaborators give you leverage? A **T-shaped** profile (deep in one of statistical genetics, structural biology, single-cell biology, or ML methods; competent across the others) is the most robust.

**Collaborate across the experiment boundary.** The highest-rung claims (C3–C4) need wet-lab partners. Offer something specific: a ranked list with a pre-registered decision rule, a calibrated uncertainty, a design that maximizes information (Chapter 46). Respect the cost of experiments; ask for the cheapest decisive one.

**Stay current without being swept along.** Follow a small set of sources (preprint servers; a few journals; conference tracks in computational biology and machine learning for biology); apply the three-pass protocol (Chapter 57); keep the reading log; replicate before you build on.

**Self-assessment.** The twelve-question capability (the research-skills page) can be scored yourself: for a recent paper or project, write answers to the twelve questions and have a colleague critique them. Gaps in your own answers (most often 6: *what information is missing*; 10: *what result would change my belief*) indicate what to practice.

## 59.7 Capstone projects

Each capstone is a twelve-week research project whose deliverable is a **short paper** with a claim–evidence table, ceilings, baselines, and a limitations section, plus a reproducible repository. They are chosen so that each has *a known-answer toy* (week 1–2), a *real-data analysis* (weeks 3–8), and *a decisive evaluation* (weeks 9–12). They are not exercises with solutions; their outcome is genuinely uncertain, and a well-supported negative result is a success.

| # | Capstone | Core question | Anchors in the book | Success criterion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **C1** | **Within-gene variant-effect harness** | For a published sequence-to-function model, how well does it explain *within-gene, across-individual* variation and fine-mapped sign, relative to a per-gene linear model? | Chs. 26, 31, 55, 57 | Open harness with ceilings, LD-stratified results, and a stratified failure analysis |
| **C2** | **Counterfactual identifiability** | When do edit effects become identified in a sequence model, and how many MPRA-like edits fix it? | Ch. 31 toy; Ch. 46 | A phase diagram over co-occurrence and data size on real MPRA data |
| **C3** | **DNA language model leakage audit** | How much of a genomic LM's apparent performance is homology (in both orientations), repeats, or tokenization shortcuts? | Chs. 28, 32, 43 | A leakage-controlled split and re-evaluation of two published models |
| **C4** | **Coevolution vs protein language models by $N_\text{eff}$** | Does a language model's contact or fitness accuracy track $N_\text{eff}$ like a Potts model does? | Chs. 29, 34, 35 | Stratified curves with confidence intervals and an interpretation |
| **C5** | **Ceiling-normalized perturbation benchmark** | Which perturbations in a public Perturb-seq dataset have enough reliability to benchmark, and how do models rank on those? | Chs. 25, 39, 43 | A reliability-stratified benchmark and a reanalysis of at least two models |
| **C6** | **Batch-integration overcorrection** | When does an integration method remove real biology, and how can composition-aware diagnostics detect it? | Chs. 25, 30, 38 | A diagnostic metric validated on simulated and real data |
| **C7** | **Sequence-model priors for fine-mapping** | Do sequence-model scores sharpen credible sets relative to a flat prior, in simulation and on a public locus set? | Chs. 26, 31, 32 | Calibrated PIPs; coverage and credible-set size versus baseline |
| **C8** | **An open problem of your choice** | Choose one open problem from Part IX; run the Attack Sheet, triage, and execute the cheapest decisive experiment | Chs. 50–54, 55–56 | A paper that states what would change your belief and whether it did |

**Twelve-week template.** *Weeks 1–2:* literature (three-pass, reading log), prior-art log, Attack Sheet, toy with known answer. *Weeks 3–4:* data acquisition, ceilings, baseline ladder, locked splits, pre-registration. *Weeks 5–8:* main analyses, ablations, seeds, error bars. *Weeks 9–10:* stratified error analysis and the adversarial test. *Week 11:* writing from the claim–evidence table; ethics and release statement. *Week 12:* internal review by a colleague acting as a prosecutor; revisions; release.

## 59.8 Worked research examples

!!! example "Worked Research Example 59.1: A release decision with no known answer"
    **Situation.** Your group has trained a genome language model on bacterial and bacteriophage genomes with eukaryotic viruses excluded from the training data, and shows that it can generate novel, functional phage genomes against a bacterial host, with a validated 5% viability rate. A collaborator proposes releasing the weights, the training code, and a fine-tuning script for any host.

    **Question.** What should you release, and what should you do first?

    **Reasoning (the framework of §59.5.3).**

    1. *Capability.* Generation of whole functional *bacteriophage* genomes is the capability class; the closest hazard is the extension to organisms of concern. The training-data exclusion reduces but may not eliminate transfer; adversarial fine-tuning studies suggest that exclusion can be partly undone with additional data and modest compute [[S]].
    2. *Agent of harm.* Phages that infect bacteria are not a human pathogen; however, the pipeline (design, synthesize, boot up) is a general method. Evaluate whether the *specific* capability yields uplift for a harmful application beyond existing tools; consult a biosecurity expert before deciding.
    3. *Counterfactual.* What can already be done with open models and public data? If a published model already provides the capability, the marginal risk of your release is lower, but the *fine-tuning script for any host* adds a convenience that lowers a barrier.
    4. *Options.* (a) Release weights and code but not the fine-tuning script; (b) API-only access with monitoring for the generative function, and open weights for the *scoring* (likelihood) function that supports variant-effect research; (c) release evaluations and the *method* but withhold generated sequences and optimized designs; (d) delay for coordinated disclosure with synthesis providers.
    5. *Evaluate the safeguard as a hypothesis.* Commission an adversarial fine-tuning evaluation with and without the data exclusion; publish the result (a safeguard evaluation is a contribution).
    6. *Document.* An ethics and release statement describing the assessment, who was consulted, and what was decided and why.

    **Expert analysis.** There is no answer that is both safe and maximally open; the decision depends on uncertain estimates of uplift. The robust practices are: *involve experts before training the next model, separate the scoring from the generating capability, evaluate the safeguard adversarially, and choose the release that is proportionate to the evidence, revisiting as capabilities change.* What the group should *not* do is to treat the question as someone else's responsibility.

!!! example "Worked Research Example 59.2: A start-up offers a clinical polygenic-score product"
    **Situation.** A company proposes to sell a polygenic risk score for coronary disease, trained on a large European-ancestry biobank, to a health system serving a population with substantial African and South Asian ancestry. They report AUROC 0.76 in the biobank.

    **Question.** What would you require before it is deployed?

    **Reasoning.**

    1. *The claim and its rung.* AUROC 0.76 in the training population is C1. Deployment in another population requires C2 evidence (Chapter 26: tagging losses of the order of 80% of $R^2$ in a toy with identical causal effects; real losses reported across ancestry [[S]]).
    2. *Incremental value.* The relevant quantity is $\Delta$AUROC or net reclassification over *clinical covariates* (age, sex, lipids, blood pressure), not the AUROC of the score alone.
    3. *Calibration in subgroups.* Predicted risk must match observed risk within ancestry groups and across the continuum of genetic similarity; a miscalibrated score leads to over- or under-treatment.
    4. *Prospective, local validation* in the target population, with a pre-specified analysis and decision threshold; *decision-curve analysis*.
    5. *Equity and consent.* Does the product widen disparities (accurate for some groups, noisy for others)? How are data, consent, and results shared with the community?
    6. *Regulation and responsibility.* Is the product a medical device? Who is accountable for harms?

    **Expert analysis.** The evaluation is the same as in Chapters 26 and 45, but now the stakes are clinical. A reasonable recommendation: do not deploy until local validation shows calibrated incremental benefit; invest in multi-ancestry training and fine-mapped, functionally informed scores; and report subgroup performance transparently.

## 59.9 Researcher's Notebook

!!! notebook "Researcher's Notebook: a pre-submission checklist"
    **Claims.** Is there a claim–evidence table? Do abstract verbs match rungs? Is every number in the text traceable to a table or figure?

    **Design.** Pre-specified analysis? Locked split with near-duplicate removal (both orientations for DNA)? Units of replication and effective $n$ stated? Multiple comparisons handled?

    **Ceilings and baselines.** Noise ceiling reported? Baseline ladder with equal tuning? Compute-matched comparison? Simple baselines (mean, size, additive, control) in the main table?

    **Statistics.** Error bars over the correct unit, over seeds *and* data resamples? Stratified results by similarity, effect size, mappability, depth, ancestry?

    **Interpretation.** Attributions supported by interventions? Causal language only with identification? Limitations as testable boundaries?

    **Reproducibility.** Code, data, environment, seeds, checkpoints; a script that regenerates the main table; documentation (datasheet, model card, DOME).

    **Ethics and safety.** Data use authorized under consent; privacy risks assessed; equity analysis; dual-use assessment recorded; release plan proportionate; institutional review sought where needed.

    **What it teaches.** The checklist is the last gate; it is quick if the study was designed for it and painful if not, which is the reason to read it first.

    **An open question to carry forward.** Can *evaluation of safeguards* be made as rigorous, public, and cumulative as benchmark evaluation for capabilities, without publishing hazardous details? What would a "ceiling" and a "baseline" mean for a biosecurity evaluation (the best attack versus the strongest defense), and who should run it?

!!! takeaways "Key takeaways"
    1. A credible study is **designed**: pre-specified analysis, locked splits, ceilings, a baseline ladder, correct units of replication, and a reproducible pipeline.
    2. Power analysis shows that typical differences between neighboring models (0.02–0.04 Spearman) are below the detectable effect for 40-unit benchmarks ($\approx0.066$ at $s=0.15$); use paired bootstraps over units and report what cannot be resolved.
    3. **Write at the rung of the evidence**: *predicts*, *is associated with*, *is consistent with*, *causes*, *designs*, with the claim–evidence table as the spine.
    4. **Human data and equity**: genotypes are identifying; respect consent; evaluate across ancestry as a continuum; adopt CARE and FAIR principles; prospective local validation for clinical tools.
    5. **Dual-use**: design capability is real (about 300 generated phage genomes, 16 viable); safeguards are partial (screening missed about a quarter of AI-redesigned toxins until patched, about 3% afterwards; data exclusion helps but can be undone by fine-tuning); apply a proportionate, documented decision framework and involve experts early.
    6. **Evaluate safeguards as hypotheses**, adversarially, and publish the evaluations.
    7. **Independence** comes from triage, collaboration across the wet-lab boundary, a reading log, and self-assessment against the twelve questions.
    8. The **eight capstones** (variant-effect harness, counterfactual identifiability, leakage audit, coevolution vs language models, perturbation benchmark, integration overcorrection, fine-mapping priors, your open problem) are twelve-week research projects; a well-supported negative result is a success.
